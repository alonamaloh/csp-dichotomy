\section{What is being proved}\label{sec:target}

\subsection{The theorem}

Fix a finite set $A$ and a finite set $\Gamma$ of relations on $A$, the
\emph{constraint language}. An instance of $\CSP(\Gamma)$ is a conjunction
$R_{1}(\bar x_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge R_{s}(\bar x_{s})$ with each
$R_{i}\in\Gamma$; the question is whether it is satisfiable.

\begin{definition}[Core, rigid core]\label{def:core}
$\Gamma$ is a \emph{core} if every endomorphism of $\Gamma$ --- every unary
polymorphism --- is an automorphism, and a \emph{rigid core} if the identity is
its only endomorphism. Write $\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}}$ for $\Gamma$ together with
the unary relation $\{a\}$ for every $a\in A$.
\end{definition}

The theorem this document is about is the rigid-core one.

\begin{theorem}[Dichotomy for a rigid core; Bulatov, Zhuk]\label{thm:dichotomy-core}
Let $\Gamma$ be a finite constraint language on a finite set $A$ containing the
singleton relation $\{a\}$ for every $a\in A$. If some weak near-unanimity
operation preserves $\Gamma$ then $\CSP(\Gamma)$ is solvable in polynomial
time; otherwise $\CSP(\Gamma)$ is \textup{NP}-complete.
\end{theorem}

The hypothesis is exactly what makes $\Pol(\Gamma)$ idempotent, since a
polymorphism preserving $\{a\}$ satisfies $f(a,\dots,a)=a$; so
Convention~\ref{conv:standing} applies to it, and \S\ref{sec:hardness} may use
the singletons as relations. It also makes $\Gamma$ a rigid core, and
conversely: by \cite[Thm.~1.4.6]{BradyNotes}, $\Gamma$ is a rigid core if and
only if every $\{a\}$ is pp-definable from $\Gamma$, so requiring the
singletons outright costs nothing beyond pp-definability.

Two imported statements carry Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy-core} to an arbitrary
$\Gamma$.

\begin{theorem}[Cores and idempotent reducts; imported]\label{thm:core-reduction}
\begin{enumerate}\alphlabels\tightlist
\item Every finite $\Gamma$ is homomorphically equivalent to a core
  $\Gamma^{\circ}$ on a subset of $A$, unique up to isomorphism, and
  $\CSP(\Gamma)$ and $\CSP(\Gamma^{\circ})$ have the same satisfiable instances
  up to a syntactic translation \cite[Prop.~1.4.3]{BradyNotes}.
\item If $\Gamma$ is a core then $\CSP(\Gamma)$ and
  $\CSP(\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}})$ are equivalent under logspace reductions
  \cite[Thm.~1.4.7]{BradyNotes}.
\item If $\Gamma$ is a core then $\Pol(\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}})$ is exactly the
  set of idempotent members of $\Pol(\Gamma)$, and every $f\in\Pol(\Gamma)$
  factors as $f=f_{\mathrm{un}}\circ f_{\mathrm{id}}$ with
  $f_{\mathrm{un}}(x)=f(x,\dots,x)$ an automorphism and
  $f_{\mathrm{id}}=f_{\mathrm{un}}^{-1}\circ f$ idempotent
  \cite[Prop.~1.4.10, 1.4.11]{BradyNotes}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[The criterion descends to the rigid core]\label{lem:wnu-descends}
Let $\Gamma$ be finite and $\Gamma^{\circ}$ a core of it. Then some weak
near-unanimity operation preserves $\Gamma$ if and only if some weak
near-unanimity operation preserves $(\Gamma^{\circ})^{\mathrm{rig}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $e\colon\Gamma^{\circ}\to\Gamma$ and $r\colon\Gamma\to\Gamma^{\circ}$ be
the homomorphisms of Theorem~\ref{thm:core-reduction}(a). If $w$ is a weak
near-unanimity polymorphism of $\Gamma$ then $\bar w=r\circ w|_{(A^{\circ})^{k}}$
is one of $\Gamma^{\circ}$: it preserves the relations, being a composite of
homomorphisms, and post-composition with $r$ preserves each identity
$w(y,x,\dots,x)=\dots=w(x,\dots,x,y)$. Conversely if $w$ is one of
$\Gamma^{\circ}$ then $e\circ w\circ(r,\dots,r)$ is one of $\Gamma$, by the
same two observations.

So we may assume $\Gamma=\Gamma^{\circ}$ is a core, and must pass between
$\Gamma$ and $\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}}$. Since $\Gamma\subseteq
\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}}$, every polymorphism of $\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}}$ is one of
$\Gamma$. In the other direction let $w\in\Pol(\Gamma)$ be a weak
near-unanimity operation and factor it as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:core-reduction}(c). Applying the automorphism
$w_{\mathrm{un}}^{-1}$ to each of the identities
$w(y,x,\dots,x)=\dots=w(x,\dots,x,y)$ leaves them equalities, so
$w_{\mathrm{id}}$ is again a weak near-unanimity operation; it is idempotent,
hence a polymorphism of $\Gamma^{\mathrm{rig}}$ by
Theorem~\ref{thm:core-reduction}(c).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Dichotomy; Bulatov, Zhuk]\label{thm:dichotomy}
Let $\Gamma$ be a finite constraint language. If some weak near-unanimity
operation preserves $\Gamma$ then $\CSP(\Gamma)$ is solvable in polynomial
time; otherwise $\CSP(\Gamma)$ is \textup{NP}-complete.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Gamma^{\circ}$ be a core of $\Gamma$ and
$\Delta=(\Gamma^{\circ})^{\mathrm{rig}}$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:core-reduction}(a),(b), $\CSP(\Gamma)$ and $\CSP(\Delta)$ are
equivalent under logspace reductions, so each is in polynomial time exactly
when the other is, and each is \textup{NP}-complete exactly when the other is.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:wnu-descends} the hypothesis holds for $\Gamma$ exactly when
it holds for $\Delta$, and $\Delta$ satisfies the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy-core}.
\end{proof}

\begin{hazard}[What the reduction is not]\label{haz:core}
Corollary~\ref{thm:dichotomy} is the form the criterion is usually quoted in,
and it is correct, but the route to it is not a formality and two points are
worth isolating.

\emph{Idempotence is the whole of it.} What
Lemma~\ref{lem:wnu-descends} has to supply, once one is at a core, is an
\emph{idempotent} weak near-unanimity operation, and it gets one only from the
factorisation of Theorem~\ref{thm:core-reduction}(c) --- available because a
core's unary polymorphisms form a group. The corresponding statement for
arbitrary nontrivial systems of identities is \textbf{false}: Willard's example
\cite[Ex.~1.4.5]{BradyNotes} is a core whose polymorphisms satisfy identities
unsatisfiable by projections while its rigidification has no such
polymorphisms at all, and is \textup{NP}-complete. Weak near-unanimity survives
the passage; ``satisfies some nontrivial identities'' does not.

\emph{The special operation.} Everything after \S\ref{sec:conventions} works
not with an arbitrary weak near-unanimity operation but with a \emph{special}
one of some arity $n$, supplied by Lemma~\ref{lem:special-wnu}. That is a
change of hypothesis and needs one sentence: the special operation is a
\emph{term} operation of $\Pol(\Gamma)$, so it is itself a polymorphism and
preserves every relation of $\Gamma$; and it is idempotent because
$\Pol(\Gamma)$ is. So $\mathbf A=(A;w)$ satisfies
Convention~\ref{conv:standing} and every relation of $\Gamma$ is a subuniverse
of a power of $\mathbf A$, which is what \S\S\ref{sec:instances} onwards
assume.

Theorem~\ref{thm:core-reduction} is imported and not proved here.
\end{hazard}

\subsection{The five layers}

Both halves of Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy-core} are conditional on a model of
computation, and that is not a formality to wave through. ``Solvable in
polynomial time'' quantifies over an encoding of instances as strings, a
machine, and a polynomial bound on its step count; ``\textup{NP}-complete''
additionally quantifies over all problems in \textup{NP} and over
polynomial-time many-one reductions. The theorem therefore splits into
statements that mention a machine and statements that do not, and the split
does not agree with the tractable/hard split.

Write $\mathbf A=(A;w)$ for a finite idempotent algebra whose basic operation
$w$ is a special weak near-unanimity operation, and $\Vn$ for the class of such
algebras with $w$ of arity $n$ (Convention~\ref{conv:standing}).

\paragraph{Tractable half.} Three layers.

\begin{description}\tightlist
\item[\textup{(T0)} \emph{The algebraic core.}] The three theorems of
  \S\ref{sec:main-statements} --- the existence of a strong subuniverse or a
  linear congruence, the safety of a reduction to a strong subuniverse, and the
  codimension-one theorem --- together with everything they rest on. These are
  statements about finite algebras, congruences, and CSP instances regarded as
  finite combinatorial objects. No computation appears in them.
\item[\textup{(T1)} \emph{Correctness of the algorithm.}] Zhuk's algorithm
  written as a total function $\textsc{Solve}$ from instances to Booleans, and
  the proposition
  \[
    \textsc{Solve}(\mathcal I)=\texttt{true}\iff
    \mathcal I\ \text{has a solution}.
  \]
  This is a statement about a recursive function and is provable from
  \textup{(T0)} with no cost model.
\item[\textup{(T2)} \emph{The complexity wrapper.}] That $\textsc{Solve}$ runs
  in polynomial time in the size of the instance, for fixed $\Gamma$. This is
  the one place a cost model is needed on this side. It is stated here and not
  proved.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Hard half.} Two layers.

\begin{description}\tightlist
\item[\textup{(H0)} \emph{The algebraic core.}] If no weak near-unanimity
  operation preserves $\Gamma$ then $\Gamma$ primitively positively interprets
  every finite constraint language --- in particular one whose CSP is
  \textup{NP}-hard. This is again pure algebra: the Galois connection between
  polymorphisms and primitive positive definability, the Maróti--McKenzie
  theorem that a Taylor term yields a weak near-unanimity term, and the
  Bulatov--Jeavons--Krokhin reduction.
\item[\textup{(H1)} \emph{The complexity wrapper.}] That a primitive positive
  interpretation yields a polynomial-time many-one reduction, and hence
  \textup{NP}-hardness. This does need a program, but the program performs a
  syntactic substitution of formulas whose size is linear in the instance, so
  it is genuinely small.
\end{description}

\textup{(T0)}, \textup{(T1)} and \textup{(H0)} are the mathematics, and are
what the literature actually proves. This document is written for them.
\textup{(T2)} and \textup{(H1)} are stated precisely in
\S\S\ref{sec:algorithm}--\ref{sec:hardness} and are not proved here; recovering
the word \emph{complete} in Theorem~\ref{thm:dichotomy-core} also needs the
standard fact that a fixed finite-template CSP lies in \textup{NP}, which
belongs with them and is Theorem~\ref{thm:np-membership}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:layers-not-a-dodge}
The separation is not a dodge. Layer \textup{(T1)} is strictly stronger than
``\,$\CSP(\Gamma)$ is decidable'', which is trivial for a finite template: it
says that one particular, highly non-obvious algorithm --- the one whose
existence is the content of the dichotomy --- is correct. Everything that makes
the dichotomy hard lives in \textup{(T0)}. Conversely, no part of
\textup{(T0)}--\textup{(T1)} becomes easier if a cost model is available.
\end{remark}
